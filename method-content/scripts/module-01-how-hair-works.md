# Segment 1, A follicle is a small organ (~3 minutes)

A hair follicle is a small organ. That is the part most of us were not taught.

A follicle has its own blood supply. It has its own immune behavior. It has its own cycle that runs on a clock that is separate from the rest of your body. There are roughly a hundred thousand follicles on the average scalp. Each one is alive, each one is on its own timeline, and each one responds to the inputs you give it.

The cycle has three phases. The growing phase, which lasts somewhere between two and seven years for a healthy follicle. The transition phase, a few weeks. The resting phase, several months. At the end of the resting phase, the hair sheds, and the cycle starts over.

This is why losing somewhere around fifty to a hundred hairs a day is normal. Your follicles are not malfunctioning. They are cycling. Some of them are mid-growth, some of them are resting, some of them are letting go. What you see in the shower is the resting-phase follicles releasing the hair they have been holding on to. The follicle itself is not gone. It is preparing for the next round.

What is not normal is when too many follicles enter the resting phase at the same time. That is called telogen effluvium, and we will talk about it in Module 5 when we get to stress. For now, what I want you to know is that hair is not built like skin. Skin renews quickly. Hair runs on a long slow timeline. Whatever you did to your hair four months ago is the hair you are looking at today.

This is the reason the Method is a year. Hair takes a year to tell you what it is going to tell you.

# Segment 2, What feeds the follicle (~3 minutes)

The follicle is a small organ, and like every organ in your body, it needs blood, oxygen, and nutrients.

Blood. The follicle sits in a tiny bed of capillaries. Anything that affects circulation affects the follicle. Cold scalps, tight ponytails, certain medications, stress, posture, sleep. Circulation is one of the four levers we will return to throughout this course.

Oxygen. The follicle wants steady oxygen delivery, which is downstream of circulation, lung function, and iron. Iron is the carrier. We will spend a lot of time on iron in Module 4. For now, hold the thought that hair-quality complaints are often, underneath, oxygen-delivery complaints, and oxygen-delivery complaints are often iron complaints.

Nutrients. The follicle needs amino acids to build keratin, which is the structural protein of hair. It needs B-vitamins to power the cellular machinery that turns those amino acids into keratin. It needs trace minerals like copper, zinc, and selenium to keep the enzymes working. It needs methionine and tyrosine to make pigment. It needs vitamin D to function. None of this is exotic. All of it is the basic chemistry of how a small organ stays alive.

When any one of those inputs runs low, the follicle responds. Sometimes by slowing the growing phase. Sometimes by shedding earlier. Sometimes by producing finer, less pigmented hair. Sometimes, if the deficit is severe, by going dormant for a long stretch.

The miss the medical system makes, and the miss I made for years, is treating hair as a surface complaint. The follicle is a small organ. You feed it the way you feed every other small organ. From the inside.

# Segment 3, The He Shou Wu heritage (~4 minutes)

I want to tell you about the herb at the heart of Folicelle Foundation. This part is not biology. This part is heritage.

The herb is called Polygonum multiflorum. In English you might see it as Chinese knotweed or fleeceflower root. In traditional Chinese medicine it has been called He Shou Wu for over a thousand years. He Shou Wu translates roughly to "Black-Haired Mr. He."

The story goes that a man named Mr. He, in his fifties, was given the root by a Taoist hermit. Mr. He's hair, which had been white, returned to its natural color. He went on to live a long life, and the herb became one of the most prized tonic plants in the Chinese herbal canon. Tonic plants are the category of herbs traditional Chinese medicine reserves for the long, slow work of supporting the body over time. He Shou Wu sits at the top of that category for hair, longevity, and what the canon describes as essence.

I want to be careful here. I am telling you a heritage story, not a clinical study. The legend of Mr. He is a legend. What is not a legend is that He Shou Wu has been used continuously for over a millennium in traditional Chinese medicine for hair concerns, that modern research has begun to investigate its compounds, and that practitioners I deeply respect consider it the heritage anchor of any serious Eastern hair tonic.

When I was building Foundation, I did not want to formulate another B-vitamin gummy with biotin on the label. I wanted to bring an Eastern heritage ingredient to a Western clinical-strength formulation. He Shou Wu is the bridge.

# Segment 4, How to read your own hair (~2 minutes)

A few things to start noticing this week.

Density at your part. The part line is the easiest place to read density change because it is consistent. Take your baseline photo at the part now. Take it again at the end of every quarter. The Toolkit will prompt you.

Density at your crown. Use the mirror on your phone, hold it up over your head, take the photo from above. The crown is where female pattern hair change shows up earliest and is the hardest to see on your own.

The diameter of your ponytail. Most stylists already know this trick. Wrap a hair tie around your full ponytail. Measure the diameter, or just feel the heft. Notice if the heft is changing month over month.

Your shed. Look at the shower drain. Look at the hairs in your brush. Counting is impossible, but pattern recognition is not. You will know if the volume changes.

The most important thing is to write your observations down. Memory is unreliable on a year-long timeline. The Toolkit gives you a place to log this. Use it.
