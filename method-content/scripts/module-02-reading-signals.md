# Cold open (~20 seconds)

Most women I talk to are not sure if what they are noticing is real.

The shower drain looks worse than it used to. The ponytail feels thinner. Or maybe it does not. Maybe you are imagining it. Maybe your friend is right and everyone sheds in fall.

This module is short. It is the lens. By the end of it, you will know what to actually look at.

*B-roll suggestion: a close on hair in a shower drain (tasteful, not graphic), then a ponytail, then a part line. Quick cuts. Title card: "Module 2, Reading Your Body's Signals."*

# Segment 1, Shedding versus thinning (~2 minutes)

These are not the same thing. The Method treats them differently.

Shedding is volume. It is how many hairs you are losing per day. The benchmark is roughly fifty to a hundred per day for a healthy adult. You will not count them. What you are looking for is a change in your own baseline. If your shower drain used to be a little wad of hair and is now a noticeable handful, your shedding has shifted. That is data.

Shedding tends to be the symptom of an event. A baby. A surgery. A diet shift. A medication. A virus. A few months of high stress. The shed shows up two to four months after the event, not during it. That delay is the most disorienting thing about shedding. You feel fine in March, and the shedding starts in May.

Thinning is density. It is how much hair you have at any given point of your scalp. Thinning is slower. It shows up at the part line, at the crown, sometimes at the temples. The hair itself often gets finer in diameter before density drops, so you might feel the heft of your ponytail change before you see the part widen.

Thinning is the symptom of a longer story. Hormonal shifts. Chronic low-grade nutritional gaps. Genetic predisposition. The kind of inputs that compound over years, not months.

You can have one. You can have both. Most women going through perimenopause or coming out of postpartum have both at the same time, and the two are tangled together. The Method does not require you to diagnose yourself. It requires you to notice which one is the loudest signal right now.

# Segment 2, Four things to look at (~2 minutes)

I want to give you the four observations that actually tell you something.

The part. Stand in front of a mirror in good light. Make your part the way you usually wear it. Take a phone photo straight down at the part. The Toolkit has a baseline photo prompt for this. Repeat it every ninety days. Year over year, the part either widens or it does not. The photo will not lie.

The crown. This is the spot at the top back of your head. It is the hardest to see on yourself. Use the camera on your phone, hold it up over your head, face down, and take the photo. The Toolkit has a prompt for this too. The crown is where female pattern hair change shows up early.

The ponytail. Wrap a hair tie around your full ponytail at the base. Note how many wraps you can do. Or measure the diameter with a tape measure if you want to be precise. Ponytail diameter is the most quietly diagnostic measurement most women have ever taken, and almost no one has taken it.

The shed. Pay attention to the shower drain, the brush, the pillow. Photograph what you see in the shower drain once a week for the first month of the year. The point is not the gram-weight of the shed. The point is whether the pattern is shifting.

These are four observations. They take less than two minutes a week. They are the inputs that turn "I think something is happening to my hair" into "I can tell you what shifted between January and April."

# Segment 3, What you might be missing (~2 minutes)

I want to name two things that women tend to miss when they start watching their hair.

The first is that the texture might change before the volume does. Your hair becoming finer, less pigmented, more fragile, more brittle. These are not separate problems. They are the same follicle responding to the same inputs, expressing it through a different signal. If your hair is harder to style than it used to be, or holding curl less well, or breaking shorter than it used to, that is the follicle telling you something before the part widens.

The second is that this is emotional. I want to say that out loud. Watching your own hair is one of the hardest forms of attention. It is connected to how you feel about being seen. It is connected to age, to motherhood, to relationships, to work. There is a reason most women avoid the mirror at the part. The avoidance is its own form of data. If you have been avoiding the part for a year, you have probably already noticed something, and you are not crazy.

I had to walk through this myself. I knew my hairline was not as thick as I was pretending it was. I avoided the studio mirror in my own office, the one with the perfect lighting, for a long time. The thing that broke through was a CEO interview I was doing for work, and the lighting that day was honest with me. The right temple was hanging on by a thread. The hairline was further back than the one I had in my head.

That kind of noticing is part of the work. It is not vanity. It is the first input.
