# Cold open (~25 seconds)

Hair is the most legible hormonal symptom you have.

It shows up before the mood does, before the labs come back, before the doctor takes the symptoms seriously. The follicle reads your hormones in real time and writes the report on your scalp. By the time you have noticed, your follicles have been telling the story for months.

This module covers the four hormonal chapters where I see the most missed signals. Postpartum. Perimenopause. GLP-1. Thyroid. Plus a shorter section on post-pill and post-thyroid changes.

# Segment 1, Postpartum hair (~3 minutes)

I want to start with postpartum because it is the most common and the most misunderstood.

During pregnancy, estrogen extends the growing phase of the follicle. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Your hair stays in growth longer than usual. The cycle slows. Many women go through pregnancy with the thickest hair of their lives. That thickness is not the new normal. It is borrowed.

After delivery, estrogen drops. [CLINICAL REVIEW] All the follicles that had been held in the extended growing phase release at roughly the same time. Two to four months after birth, you start losing hair in volume. Handfuls. Shower drains. Pillowcases. It can be terrifying. The first time it happens to you, no one prepares you for it.

This is postpartum telogen effluvium. [CLINICAL REVIEW] It is, in the strictest sense, normal. The body is returning the borrowed follicles. Most women see the heaviest shedding from roughly three months postpartum to roughly six to nine months postpartum, with the shed tapering through the end of the first year.

*[Editor: cut in Safahi from Episode 1, around 4:42 to 5:10, describing telogen effluvium as a major-event-triggered shedding that resolves over time. The clip reinforces Dafina's framing without contradicting it. CLINICAL REVIEW of the chosen clip required.]*

What the medical system gets wrong about postpartum hair is that it tells women to wait it out, which is mostly correct, while missing the secondary contributors that are within the woman's control. Postpartum women are often iron-deficient. They are often vitamin-D deficient. They are often thyroid-shifting. They are often not sleeping. They are often not eating enough. They are often on prescription medications that affect hair. Telling them "it will come back" is true and also useless.

What I want you to do, if you are in a postpartum chapter, is to run the labs we talk about in Module 7. Ferritin. Full thyroid panel. Vitamin D. B-12 and folate. The labs are even more valuable in postpartum than at any other time because the body has been under unusual load and the deficiencies show up. [CLINICAL REVIEW]

The hair will come back. It will come back faster and fuller if your iron is not at seven, your thyroid is supported, and your vitamin D is in range.

# Segment 2, Perimenopause hair (~4 minutes)

Perimenopause is the chapter that surprises most women, because the conversation around it has been quiet for far too long.

The hormonal shifts of perimenopause start in the mid-thirties for some women and the mid-forties for many. The dominant pattern is that estrogen and progesterone become more variable. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Some months are higher, some are lower, the pattern is less predictable than it was in your twenties. As this happens, androgens, which include testosterone, become relatively more dominant by ratio, even if your testosterone numbers do not change. [CLINICAL REVIEW]

The follicle feels this ratio shift. In women genetically predisposed to female pattern hair change, also called androgenetic hair change, the follicles at the part line, the crown, and the temples become more sensitive to androgens. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Those follicles miniaturize over time. The strand gets finer. The growing phase shortens. The hair you grow becomes less dense and less pigmented.

*[Editor: cut in Safahi from Episode 1, around 4:05 to 4:40, where she describes female pattern hair loss as widening of the part line in women, and recommends getting to a dermatologist who specializes in hair as early as possible. CLINICAL REVIEW of the chosen clip.]*

What you might notice in perimenopause. Wider part. Crown that catches the light differently than it used to. Temples that look further back. Hair that does not hold a curl as well. Hair that breaks shorter. A persistent feeling that your ponytail is thinner. Mood and sleep and weight changes that are happening at the same time, all of which feed back into the follicle.

What the medical system gets wrong about perimenopause is that the conversation often jumps to hormone replacement therapy as the answer, without first reading the layers underneath. HRT is the right answer for many women. It is not the only answer. The labs I want you to run before you make decisions about HRT are the same five we will cover in Module 7. Plus a fuller hormone panel for your stage.

I will not give you a position on HRT in this module. The optional bonus module in July covers HRT, GLP-1, and hair loss specifics in more depth, and Dr. Safahi reviews that module. What I will tell you here is that if you are in perimenopause and noticing hair changes, hair is the alarm. The fire underneath might be hormonal, might be nutritional, might be thyroid, might be all three. The work is to read the signal carefully before you commit to a treatment plan.

One more thing on perimenopause. There is a category of treatment that gets sold to perimenopausal women, especially in med spa contexts, that I want you to be cautious of. Testosterone pellet programs. [CLINICAL REVIEW] These are subcutaneous testosterone pellets, often marketed for energy, libido, and mood. They are also lucrative recurring programs for the clinics that offer them. I was offered one of these programs by a Westport practitioner. Testosterone pellets are known to accelerate hair loss in genetically predisposed women. [CLINICAL REVIEW] I declined. I would encourage you to ask very specific questions before saying yes to a testosterone pellet program, and to bring the doctor script to that conversation.

# Segment 3, GLP-1 and hair (~3 minutes)

GLP-1 medications, which include Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, Zepbound, and the related class, are the fastest-growing category of weight-loss medications in the country. They work. Many of the women I know are on them or have been on them. There is also a hair conversation that nobody is having with them at the prescribing visit.

What appears to be happening, and the clinical conversation is still catching up to this, is that rapid weight loss on GLP-1 medications produces a downstream hair shed for many users. [CLINICAL REVIEW] The shed is usually telogen effluvium, the same kind of shed as postpartum or post-major-event, triggered by the metabolic shift and the reduced caloric intake. Three to four months after starting the medication, the shed begins. Three to four months after the weight stabilizes, the shed usually slows. [CLINICAL REVIEW]

*[Editor: cut in Safahi from Episode 1, around 6:29 to 6:54, where she describes anecdotally and now with growing evidence that GLP-1 users experience hair loss often tied to nutritional deficiencies that come with the medication. CLINICAL REVIEW of the chosen clip.]*

The contributing factors are real and within your control if you know to look. Most GLP-1 users are eating less protein, less iron-rich food, less of almost everything. That is the mechanism of the medication. The shed is partly the metabolic shift and partly the under-supply of the building blocks the follicle is asking for.

What to do if you are on a GLP-1 and noticing a shed.

Run the labs. Ferritin, vitamin D, B-12, folate, and a full thyroid panel. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Almost every GLP-1 user I have talked to who has run the labs comes back with at least one number that needs attention.

Increase your protein intake intentionally. Even when you are not hungry, hit the daily protein target your practitioner recommends for your weight. Hair is a protein. The follicle is not building keratin if you are not eating amino acids.

Talk to your prescriber about the dosing pace. Sometimes a slower titration reduces the shedding signal. Always defer to your prescriber on dose decisions.

Add the daily ritual that supplies the nutritional inputs the follicle is missing. This is where Foundation makes the most sense for GLP-1 users. The Western clinical-strength formulation was built for this kind of under-supply. I will not push the supplement in this module. I will tell you that if you are on a GLP-1 and your protocol from Module 8 recommends Foundation, the GLP-1 chapter is a clear use case.

# Segment 4, Thyroid (~2 minutes)

The thyroid is the most underdiagnosed contributor to hair changes in women, and the most fixable.

The standard thyroid screen is TSH. [CLINICAL REVIEW] TSH alone misses a lot. Most women who walk in with subclinical thyroid issues have TSH numbers that are within the normal range, with abnormal T3, T4, or thyroid antibodies. [CLINICAL REVIEW]

*[Editor: cut in Safahi from Episode 1, around 44:48 to 45:30, where she names thyroid disorders as a major contributor to hair loss and notes the "quasi subclinical thyroid disease" pattern that many women fall into. CLINICAL REVIEW of the chosen clip.]*

The full thyroid panel I would ask for is TSH, free T3, free T4. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Reverse T3 if your practitioner will run it. Thyroid antibodies (TPO and TgAb) if there is family history of autoimmunity or you have other autoimmune markers.

What hair changes look like in thyroid disorder. Often a diffuse thinning across the whole scalp, not just at the part. Brittle, dry hair. Sometimes the outer third of the eyebrow thins, which is a tell that experienced clinicians look for. Fatigue, weight changes, mood shifts, temperature sensitivity that does not match the season.

Thyroid is one of the chapters where the right intervention can change everything within months. The right intervention is a medical conversation. The Method gives you the framework. Your practitioner gives you the prescription if one is needed.

# Segment 5, Post-pill and post-thyroid (~1.5 minutes)

Two shorter chapters to name.

Post-pill. If you came off hormonal birth control in the last two years and your hair has been doing something unusual, that is real. [CLINICAL REVIEW] The pill modulates hormones in ways that affect the follicle. Coming off the pill produces its own hormonal recalibration. Hair shedding three to six months after stopping the pill is documented. [CLINICAL REVIEW] The shed usually settles within nine to twelve months as your cycle returns to baseline. The labs are still worth running.

Post-thyroid. If you have started, stopped, or changed thyroid medication, the follicle responds. Sometimes the shedding starts when the medication starts, even if the medication is the right one. Sometimes the shedding starts when an under-medicated body is finally getting the right dose. [CLINICAL REVIEW] The timeline is similar to other hormonal chapters. Three to four months for the shift to show up, three to four months to settle.

Both chapters benefit from the same framework. Read the signal. Run the labs. Make the protocol. Wait the year.
