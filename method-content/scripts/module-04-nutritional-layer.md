# Segment 1, The ferritin-seven story (~4 minutes)

I want to tell you the full story, because the lesson is in the timeline.

In February of the year before I built Folicelle, I went to Verbier for a ski trip. I had been feeling rough for weeks. Lightheaded. A cough I could not shake. No energy. I decided to swing into Medi Verbier just to make sure I did not have a sinus infection or some kind of altitude-related thing.

The doctor I met with was Dr. Sabine. She spent two hours with me. She did a full intake. She ran a comprehensive blood panel. Two days later, the results came back.

My ferritin level was seven. [CLINICAL REVIEW] The bottom of the normal range, depending on the lab, is around twenty. For most clinicians who work on hair, the number you want is well above that. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Mine was at seven. I had been losing hair, losing energy, losing weight, and bouncing between specialists for over a year.

Dr. Sabine asked me one question. Have you had any inklings.

I said yes. My hair.

She started me on an iron infusion that same week. [CLINICAL REVIEW] She had me return in eight weeks for repeat labs and a stool sample, to rule out a gastrointestinal cause of the iron deficiency. She put together a long-term plan that would likely include iron infusions once a year or every other year, given how I was eating and how I cycle.

What I learned in those two hours that the four practitioners in the United States had missed.

Iron is the carrier of oxygen in your blood. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Ferritin is the storage form of iron. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Your iron level on a standard panel can come back normal while your ferritin is in the basement, because ferritin is the long-term reserve and the blood level reflects what you are circulating right now. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Your follicles live on the reserve. By the time the blood level drops, the follicle has been undersupplied for a long time.

The common contributors to low ferritin in women are heavy menstrual cycles, low intake of iron-rich food (especially in vegetarians, pescatarians, and women who avoid red meat), and gastrointestinal absorption issues. [CLINICAL REVIEW] I was a pescatarian. I had been one for over a decade. No nutritionist I had seen had ever told me that pescatarian women need to be intentional about iron absorption.

After my second visit to Switzerland in April, my ferritin had moved from seven to two hundred fifteen. [CLINICAL REVIEW] The stool sample came back clean. We isolated the combination of my diet and my cycle as the contributors. My hair did not regrow immediately, because hair is on a year-long timeline, but the inputs were finally there.

The lesson is not that you need to fly to Switzerland. The lesson is that the appointment Dr. Sabine ran is available to you in your own city, if you walk in knowing what to ask for. The first thing to ask for is ferritin.

Low vitamin D affects mood, immune function, bone health, and hair. # Segment 3, What the follicle is actually building from (~3 minutes)

The labs tell you whether the inputs are there. This segment tells you what the inputs are for.

Iron and oxygen. The follicle is one of the most metabolically active tissues in your body. [CLINICAL REVIEW] It needs steady oxygen delivery to grow. Oxygen is carried by hemoglobin. Hemoglobin is built with iron. If iron is low, oxygen delivery is compromised, and follicles slow down. [CLINICAL REVIEW] This is why ferritin is the number that punches above its weight in hair-quality conversations.

Protein and amino acids. Hair is made of keratin, which is a protein. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Building keratin requires the building blocks: amino acids, especially methionine, cysteine, and tyrosine. [CLINICAL REVIEW] If you are not eating enough protein for your body size, your follicle is not building enough keratin to maintain density. The general recommendation for women is roughly point eight to one gram of protein per pound of body weight, depending on activity and stage. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Talk to a registered dietitian for your specific target.

B-vitamins. The B-complex is the cellular machinery that turns the amino acids into keratin and that supports nearly every other follicle function. [CLINICAL REVIEW] The most commonly cited for hair are B-7 (biotin), B-9 (folate), B-12, and B-6. Foundation includes therapeutic dosages of all four, with the methylated forms of folate and B-12.

Copper, zinc, selenium. The trace minerals that the enzymes inside the follicle rely on. Copper supports pigment production. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Zinc supports the structural integrity of the strand. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Selenium is an antioxidant cofactor. [CLINICAL REVIEW] The ratios matter. Too much zinc without copper can produce copper deficiency. Too much copper without zinc can produce other imbalances. Foundation is formulated with the ratio in mind. If you take a separate zinc supplement, watch the totals. [CLINICAL REVIEW]

Tyrosine. The precursor to melanin, the pigment that gives your hair color. [CLINICAL REVIEW] If you are noticing your hair losing color faster than seems right for your age, tyrosine is one of the inputs worth thinking about. The bonus modules over the course of the year may go deeper on this.

Chlorella, the green algae in Foundation, is included for its mineral profile and its absorption-supporting role. [CLINICAL REVIEW]

He Shou Wu, the Eastern heritage ingredient we walked through in Module 1, is the lineage anchor. The modern clinical conversation on He Shou Wu is still developing. The traditional use is over a thousand years deep. [CLINICAL REVIEW]

This is what your follicle is asking for. Some of it can come from food alone, if your diet, absorption, and life stage are working in your favor. For most women I know, some combination of food and supplementation is the most realistic path.

# Segment 4, A practical guide to iron (~2 minutes)

I want to spend an extra minute on iron because the rest of the module is overview and iron is where the most lives are quietly changing.

Where iron lives in food. Heme iron, from animal sources (red meat, organ meat, oysters, sardines), is the most absorbable form. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Non-heme iron, from plant sources (lentils, beans, spinach, dark chocolate, fortified cereals), is real iron but less absorbable. [CLINICAL REVIEW] Pairing non-heme iron with vitamin C in the same meal increases absorption. [CLINICAL REVIEW]

Where iron is blocked. Coffee, tea, and calcium-rich foods consumed at the same time as iron-rich foods reduce absorption. [CLINICAL REVIEW] If you are working to raise your ferritin, try not to pair your iron meal with your morning coffee.

The cast iron pot. There is a long folk tradition of cooking in cast iron to add iron to food. The contemporary version is that the iron added by cast iron cookware is measurable, but small. [CLINICAL REVIEW] It is not a replacement for dietary or supplemental iron, but it is a small, free, daily contributor that costs nothing once you own the pot.

Supplementation. Oral iron is available over the counter. It can be hard on the stomach. The forms most commonly recommended for sensitivity are ferrous bisglycinate and heme iron polypeptide. [CLINICAL REVIEW] If oral iron is not raising your ferritin and your numbers are low, talk to your practitioner about IV iron infusion, which is what I needed. [CLINICAL REVIEW]

This is not medical advice. This is the framework that will let you ask the right questions of a practitioner who is paying attention.
