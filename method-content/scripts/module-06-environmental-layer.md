# Cold open (~20 seconds)

The internet has convinced you that you need a twelve-step scalp routine.

Most of it does not move the needle on hair the way internal inputs do. Some of it does. This module separates the noise from the signal so you stop spending money on the noise.

*B-roll suggestion: a cabinet shelf full of half-used hair products, then a single bottle being lifted off the shelf. Title card: "Module 6, The Environmental Layer."*

---

# Segment 1, The four environmental inputs that actually matter (~2 minutes)

Four inputs. In order.

Water. The hardness of your water, and what is in it beyond water itself, lands on your scalp every time you wash. Hard water leaves mineral residue. Chlorinated water is rough on the scalp microbiome. A simple inline shower filter, the kind that costs less than a hundred dollars and screws onto the showerhead, will change the texture of your hair faster than almost any product you can buy. Most stylists I know agree on this. It is the first investment I would make.

Heat. This is the one most of us already know about and ignore. High direct heat damages the cuticle. The cuticle is the outer layer of the hair shaft. Damaged cuticle means breakage, dullness, frizz. The fix is not to never use heat. The fix is two things. A heat protectant before any tool that goes above two hundred degrees. And lowering the temperature setting on the tool. Most blow dryers and irons run hotter than they need to. If yours has a setting, use a lower one. Your hair will not know the difference except in how much breakage it shows you in two months.

Products. The honest version is that most hair products are formulated for what they feel like in the chair, not what they leave behind. The two questions I would ask of any product are, what is the surfactant doing to my scalp, and what is the residue doing to my follicle. You do not need a five-step routine. A clean shampoo, a conditioner that matches your hair density, a leave-in if you blow dry, and a scalp serum if you have a specific concern. That is most of what most women need.

The fourth is the one no one talks about. Wash frequency. The follicle does not thrive in sebum buildup. Sebum is the oil your scalp makes. It builds up faster than people think it does, especially under extensions, hats, dry shampoo, or a long stretch between washes. The shower microbiome on a too-rarely-washed scalp can shift toward yeast overgrowth, which produces dandruff and inflammation, and inflammation works against the follicle. Most women I know wash too infrequently because they have been told frequent washing damages hair. It does not damage hair when it is done well. Sebum buildup damages the follicle.

*B-roll suggestion: a shower filter being screwed onto a showerhead, a blow dryer's temperature dial being turned down, a shampoo label being read in good light, a clean towel pressing water out of clean hair. The whole segment is small physical actions.*

# Segment 2, A pattern by hair type (~1.5 minutes)

I want to give you a rough washing-frequency guide that comes from the women I have talked to and the practitioners I trust. This is not a rule. Hair is individual.

Fine, low-density, lightly oily hair tends to do best with washing every other day to every day. Sebum builds up fastest on fine hair because there is less hair to absorb it.

Medium density, normal-to-oily hair tends to do well at every other day to two-out-of-three days.

Thicker, drier, wavier hair tends to do well at two to three times a week. Long enough between washes for natural oils to coat the shaft, short enough to keep the scalp from going past the inflammation tipping point.

Tightly curled or coily hair has its own logic and its own community of stylists and dermatologists who know it best. Wash frequency in textured hair is informed by porosity, moisture, and protective styles. The rough range is once a week to once every two weeks. The most respectful thing I can do in a course like this is to say, listen to your own hair and to the practitioners who specialize in it.

Extension wearers, this is for you. The shame of "how often should I wash my extensions" is real and the answer most stylists I know give is, more often than you are. Sebum buildup at the bond is the same dynamic as on a bare scalp. The buildup smells. The buildup causes irritation. The buildup contributes to shedding at the bond. You do not have to wash every day. You have to wash when it needs it, and trust that your stylist sized your extensions for a hair routine that includes washing.

# Segment 3, What the internet sells that does not matter (~1.5 minutes)

A short list of things I would deprioritize until you have the four inputs above in order.

Most scalp scrubs. Scalp scrubs at high frequency can be irritating. Once every two weeks is fine. Daily is not.

Most "thickening" shampoos. They thicken the strand cosmetically. They do not affect the follicle. Some are fine to use. Do not pay a hundred dollars for them.

Most peptide serums sold at the drugstore. The active peptides at clinically meaningful concentrations are in the higher-end products and have been studied in earlier-stage trials. The bargain-tier serums often contain peptides at trace concentrations that do not do the work the label implies. If you want to try peptide serums, the quarterly bonus module in April covers the ones I would actually consider.

Most expensive heat tools, beyond a certain price point. A good blow dryer or iron at a reasonable price with adjustable temperature is most of the value. The premium beyond that is comfort and brand, not follicle outcome.

Rosemary oil. This one is actually defensible. The clinical conversation puts rosemary oil in the same category as low-strength topical minoxidil for some users. It is cheap. It is low risk. If you want to try it, dilute it in a carrier oil like castor or coconut. Apply to the scalp, not the strand. Leave on overnight, wash out in the morning. Not before washing, because then you are just rinsing it off.

The April quarterly bonus module goes deeper on all of this. For this module, the four inputs above are the framework.

# The teaching moment (~30 seconds)

If you keep one thing from this module, keep this. The environment matters less than your inputs from the inside, but the parts that matter, matter a lot.

Water filter, lower heat, fewer products, washing when you should. That is the environmental layer.

*Lower third overlay: "Fewer things, done more carefully."*
