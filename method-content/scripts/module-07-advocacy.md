# Cold open (~25 seconds)

**[On camera, Westport home, late afternoon natural light, sitting at a desk or counter, no styling busywork in the background.]**

When my ferritin came back at seven, the doctor who finally caught it was in Switzerland.

Not in New York, where I live. Not in Westport, where I raise my kids. Not in any of the four offices I had already paid out of pocket to walk into and tell my hair was thinning. The doctor who finally read what my body was telling me was in a small mountain clinic in Verbier and her name is Dr. Sabine.

This module is about why that happened. Not because the American medical system is broken in some abstract way. It's about a very specific gap that you can learn to walk into and walk back out of with answers.

I built the Method around this module. Everything else exists to make this one usable.

# Segment 1, The four rooms I sat in before Verbier (~4 minutes)

I want to tell you about four offices. Not to be unkind to anyone in them. To show you what the gap looks like.

The first office was a Harvard-trained dermatologist in Manhattan. I paid for a consultation specifically about hair loss. I sat down. I told her my crown was thinning. She ordered some blood work and looked at my hair under a light. She did not ask me about my cycle. She did not ask me about my diet. She did not ask me about stress, sleep, the last twelve months of my life, or whether I had been losing weight. She did mention that one of my numbers was low and that I might want to follow up. She offered me PRP. Five thousand dollars. She said, with a kind smile, that it would be painful and that it might not be effective.

I left. I did not get PRP.

The second office was a fancy Westport practitioner. Different office, different demeanor, same blind spot. She ran a panel that included my testosterone. My testosterone was on the low end of normal. She suggested a pellet program for testosterone, which is a recurring, lucrative, monthly thing for clinics. Pellet testosterone is also known to accelerate hair loss in women. I did not start the pellet program. I left that office too.

The third office was my hairstylist's chair. She is one of the kindest people I know. She was the first person to see it. She is the reason I started asking these questions in the first place. And the only tool she had was Rogaine. She did the best she could with what she had been given to work with, which was, professionally, not enough. She had no diagnostic training, no lab to send me to, no referral network. She handed me a bottle of minoxidil and told me to come back in six months.

The fourth office, this one is years older, was a general practitioner. I went in tired. Not hair tired. Body tired. The kind of tired where the idea of life is exhausting. I described what I was feeling. She looked at my chart and said, "You're a mom of twins. Get used to it."

That is the gap.

Four practitioners, four offices, four bills, and not one of them asked the question that would have shown my ferritin was at seven. The system is not malicious. It is fragmented. Dermatology bills by procedure. GPs run the panels you ask for, not the panels you should ask for. Stylists see the result but were never taught the cause. And in the middle of all of that is you, on a Tuesday afternoon, googling at midnight, trying to translate between five appointments that don't talk to each other.

# Segment 2, What changed in Verbier (~3 minutes)

I went to Verbier for a ski trip. I had been feeling rough for weeks. Lightheaded. A persistent cough. Energy that was just not there. I decided to swing into Medi Verbier to make sure I didn't have an infection.

I was expecting twenty minutes. I sat with Dr. Sabine for two hours.

She asked me everything. My cycle. My diet. My stress. My sleep. When my hair had started changing. She did not separate "wellness" from "illness." She asked me about my life. She ran blood work and the blood work came back with a ferritin level of seven.

Seven is extremely low. Normal at the bottom end of the range is around twenty, and for hair to actually grow, most clinicians prefer ferritin well above that. Mine was at seven and I had been bouncing between specialists for over a year.

Dr. Sabine said one thing that I will remember for the rest of my life. She said that what gets dismissed as a cosmetic concern is often a diagnostic signal for something deeper. The hair is the alarm. The hair is not the problem.

She started me on an iron infusion. Within a few days I felt different. She told me to come back in eight weeks. She told me to do a stool sample before the next visit so we could rule out a gastrointestinal cause. She made a plan that included follow-up, including labs I had never been told to ask for. None of this was exotic medicine. None of this required a small clinic in the Alps to deliver. It was time, attention, and a willingness to read hair as a signal instead of a complaint.

The flight to Switzerland is not the lesson. The lesson is that the kind of appointment Dr. Sabine ran is available to you in your own city, if you walk in knowing what to ask for.

# Segment 3, The five labs your appointment is missing (~4 minutes)

I want to give you the list. Not the entire medical literature, the five labs that the practitioners in my four offices did not run, and that Dr. Sabine ran in two hours.

You do not need to memorize these. They are written down in the doctor script the Toolkit will generate for you. You bring the script to the appointment. The appointment becomes faster, kinder, and more useful. That is the entire mechanism.

The five labs are ferritin, the full thyroid panel, vitamin D, vitamin B-12 and folate, and a sex hormone panel.

Ferritin. This is your iron storage number, not your iron blood level. The blood-level number can be normal while your ferritin is at seven. Ferritin is what your follicles are actually living on. A reasonable normal range starts somewhere around twenty, and most clinicians who work on hair want you considerably higher than that. If your practitioner runs iron and not ferritin, that is the moment to ask.

Full thyroid panel. Not just TSH. TSH alone can sit inside the normal range while you are quietly underconverting T4 to T3 and walking through your life sluggish, weight-gaining, and shedding. Ask for TSH, free T3, free T4. Reverse T3 if your practitioner will run it. Thyroid antibodies if you have any reason to think autoimmunity is in play.

Vitamin D. Most of us are low. People with darker skin tones are more likely to be low. People who work indoors are more likely to be low. People who wear sunscreen well are more likely to be low. Low vitamin D shows up in hair as much as it shows up in mood, in bones, in immune function. It is a five-dollar test and almost everyone runs it.

Vitamin B-12 and folate. The methylated forms are the ones that matter for absorption. If you happen to have a methylation issue, the standard forms in most multivitamins are not doing what the label says they are doing. You can ask your practitioner about methylation testing if you want to go further, but the simple version is to run B-12 and folate and to take the methylated forms if you are supplementing.

Sex hormones. The list depends on your stage. Postpartum, peri, post-pill, post-thyroid, GLP-1, each has a different panel. The doctor script the Toolkit generates is customized to your stage. The general principle is that hair is the most legible hormonal symptom you have. If you do not know what your hormones are doing, you do not know what your hair is doing.

These five labs are not exotic. They are not expensive. Most are covered. They are the labs your practitioner can run today. The reason they have not been run yet is almost always that no one asked for them.

# Segment 4, The doctor script and how to use it (~4 minutes)

The Doctor Script Generator is in your Toolkit. You filled out a Baseline Assessment when the course started. The generator takes those answers and produces a one-page printable script. You print it, you fold it into your bag, you walk into your next appointment with it.

The script has five sections.

A patient context paragraph. One paragraph that describes your situation in plain language. Not "I have hair loss." Something like "I have noticed increased shedding over the past six months along with a change in density at my crown and part, and I would like to use this appointment to understand whether bloodwork can help us identify a contributor."

The labs to discuss. Ranked for your stage. The reason for the rank is in the script too. Two sentences per lab. Not a medical lecture. A short, specific request.

A notes section. Blank space. You fill it in during the appointment. The notes section is doing more work than it looks like it is doing. The presence of a notes section signals that you are the person running this appointment. You came prepared. You expect to leave with something.

A required disclaimer at the bottom. The script is educational. It is not a diagnosis or a prescription. Your provider decides what is appropriate. The disclaimer protects everyone, including you, and it lowers the temperature in the room because it explicitly defers to the practitioner's judgment.

A signature line for you. You sign it. You date it. You take a photo of it on your phone before you walk in. That photo is your record of what you asked for.

Here is the hard part. The doctor script is not aggressive. It is not adversarial. It is the opposite. It is the politest, most prepared version of the appointment you have ever had. The practitioner who reads it will recognize, in five seconds, that you are a serious person with a serious question. The five-minute appointment becomes a fifteen-minute appointment. The fifteen-minute appointment becomes a referral to the lab. That referral is what you came for.

If the practitioner refuses to run the labs, that is data. You leave that office. You find a different practitioner. The script becomes evidence of what you asked for, in writing, the first time. The Toolkit lets you regenerate the script before every appointment, because your context changes. The labs change. The questions change. You change.

# Segment 5, The day after, and the next one (~3 minutes)

You will walk out of that appointment with one of three outcomes.

You will walk out with the lab orders in hand. This is the best case. You go to the lab. You get the draw. You wait three to ten days. The results come back. You enter them into the Lab Results Module of the Toolkit. The plain-language explanations are right there. You read your own numbers. You take the numbers back to the practitioner for interpretation, which is their job, not yours. You make a plan.

You will walk out with a referral. Not the worst case. Sometimes the right practitioner is not the one in front of you. A referral to a hair-focused dermatologist, an endocrinologist, an OB-GYN, a registered dietitian. The Method's Care Network discusses some at-home and local options I would recommend. None of them are formal partners. They are options I would point you to.

You will walk out with nothing. This happens. Your practitioner reads the script, declines to run the labs, and offers you something off-the-shelf. A topical, a brand-name prescription, a referral to a med spa. This is the moment that taught me to leave. There is a different practitioner for you. You print the script again. You make a different appointment. You do not blame yourself for needing a second appointment.

What I want you to know is that the writing of the script is the work. Not the labs themselves. The labs are a four-minute blood draw. The work is asking. The work is naming what you have been noticing. The work is sitting in front of a stranger and saying that your hair is telling you something and you would like a partner in reading the signal.

That is the entire posture of self-advocacy. You are not asking for a cure. You are asking for a conversation that includes the right inputs.
